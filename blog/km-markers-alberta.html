Kilometre Markers in Alberta: A Driver’s Guide

July 12, 2026 5 min read

Alberta driving splits into two worlds. One is flat, fast, and gridded — prairie highways running straight to the horizon, numbered with a logic you can navigate by. The other starts abruptly west of Calgary, where the Trans-Canada aims at a wall of Rockies and the road rules change with the elevation. Both worlds run on kilometres, and both reference locations the same way: highway, direction, and how far along you are. Here’s how Alberta’s system works, and how it’s coming to MileCheck.

Highway 2: the QEII, Alberta’s main street

Every province has one highway that carries a disproportionate share of its life. In Alberta it’s Highway 2 — the Queen Elizabeth II Highway, the divided freeway between Calgary and Edmonton. It’s about 300 kilometres city to city, it connects the province’s two big metros with Red Deer sitting almost exactly halfway, and it’s the busiest corridor in Alberta by a wide margin — commuters, freight, and the endless two-way traffic of a province whose population lives mostly along this one line. Through Calgary, the same Highway 2 wears a local name: Deerfoot Trail, the city’s main north–south freeway. It’s the same road — a traffic report about “the Deerfoot” and one about “the QEII” are talking about Highway 2 in different clothes.

On the QEII, Deerfoot, and the freeway ring roads, exit numbers follow the kilometre count — the same convention Ontario and Quebec use — so the exit number doubles as a position, and subtracting two exits gives you the distance between them.

Highway 1: the Trans-Canada into the Rockies

Alberta’s stretch of Highway 1, the Trans-Canada, is the mountain-parks run. From Calgary it heads west across ranch country, passes Canmore, enters Banff National Park, and climbs past Banff and Lake Louise to the BC border at Kicking Horse Pass — some of the most photographed driving in North America, and some of the most weather-exposed. Eastbound it’s the prairie freight route through Medicine Hat to Saskatchewan. The mountain section demands the same respect BC’s passes do: winter storms arrive fast at elevation, wildlife on the road is a genuine hazard (the parks’ famous wildlife overpasses exist for a reason), and the highway is the only realistic line through the range.

The north has its own version of the same run: Highway 16, the Yellowhead, carries the Trans-Canada’s northern branch from the Saskatchewan border through Edmonton and west to Jasper, where it crosses into BC over Yellowhead Pass — a gentler crossing than Kicking Horse, and the reason a lot of winter freight prefers it. Between the two Trans-Canada branches and the QEII connecting them, you’ve drawn the box most Alberta driving happens inside.

The grid: 1–216, and the 500s and up

Alberta’s highway numbering has a clean structure. The 1–216 series is the primary provincial network — the core routes, from the Trans-Canada (1) and the QEII (2) through the connector highways, up to the big-city ring roads: 216 is Anthony Henday Drive, Edmonton’s ring, and 201 is Stoney Trail around Calgary. Above that sit the secondary highways — the 500s, 700s, and up — the local and rural routes threading the township grid. As a rule of thumb: one- and two-digit numbers are the highways you plan a trip around; three-digit numbers starting at 500 are the roads you take to reach a farm, a lake, or a gas plant. On all of them, a location is the highway number plus a distance from a junction or town — prairie roads have long sightlines and few landmarks, which makes the kilometre count carry more of the load, not less.

Winter, the Icefields Parkway, and 511 Alberta

Alberta winter driving is less about mountain passes than BC’s — the QEII is flat — and more about wind, drifting snow, and ice at highway speed. Whiteouts on the open prairie sections can close highways with little warning, and freezing rain events glaze hundreds of kilometres at a time. The mountain exception is Highway 93, the Icefields Parkway, the Jasper–Lake Louise run past the Columbia Icefield: dazzling in summer, seriously committing in winter, with long stretches carrying no services and no cell coverage, and closures for avalanche control and storms a normal part of the season. It’s a road to check before you drive it, every time.

The check is 511 Alberta — the province’s road-report service, with closures, conditions, and cameras reported by highway and location. And when you’re the one reporting — a breakdown on the QEII shoulder, a collision on a secondary highway — the useful message is the same one every dispatcher on the continent wants: the highway, your direction, and your distance from the last town or interchange. On the freeway corridors, the last exit number you passed is that distance, because the exits count kilometres.

What this means in MileCheck

MileCheck’s job is to tell you exactly where you are, in the reference system your road actually uses. In the US that’s the mile marker. In Alberta it’s the kilometre — and Alberta support is coming to MileCheck in an upcoming update. When it lands, the app will show your live position as highway plus kilometre — Hwy 2 · KM 145-style — the way the exit signs, 511 Alberta, and the tow dispatcher already talk. Whether you’re on the QEII between the cities or somewhere on a 500-series road with nothing but canola in sight, the answer to “where are you?” will be on your screen.
