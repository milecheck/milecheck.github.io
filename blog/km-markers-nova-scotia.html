Kilometre Markers in Nova Scotia: A Driver’s Guide

July 12, 2026 5 min read

Nova Scotia is one of the Canadian provinces where you can actually see the kilometre number standing at the side of the road. On the 100-series highways — the controlled-access routes that carry nearly all of the province’s long-distance traffic — physical km markers tick past the way mileposts do on a US interstate. But the system has a sharp edge: step off the 100-series onto a trunk road, or onto the most famous drive in the province, and the markers stop entirely. Here’s where Nova Scotia’s markers live, where they don’t, and how MileCheck shows it.

The 100-series: where the markers live

Nova Scotia’s km-marked network is the 100-series: Highways 101 through 107, plus the 118 and the 125. These are the province’s divided and controlled-access arterials, and the province maintains a GPS-collected inventory of the physical marker positions along them — about 1,248 posted kilometre markers across the network. That inventory is the same data that MileCheck uses. If you’re on a highway whose number starts with a 1 and has three digits, there’s a kilometre marker somewhere nearby, and it’s the most precise location you can give anyone.

One honest caveat before the corridor tour: Nova Scotia numbers its exits sequentially, not by kilometre. Unlike Ontario or Quebec, where the exit number doubles as a distance, a Nova Scotia exit number is just a count — Exit 7 tells you it comes after Exit 6, nothing more. That makes the posted km markers matter more here, not less: they’re the only distance reference the road gives you.

Highway 102: the spine

The 102 runs about 100 kilometres from Halifax north to Truro, and it is the highway almost every Nova Scotia trip touches: it carries the city’s commuters, serves Halifax Stanfield International Airport around the 30-kilometre mark, and ends at Truro — the “hub of Nova Scotia” — where it hands off to the 104. If the province’s highway network is a wheel, the 102 is the axle.

Highway 104: the Trans-Canada, over the Cobequid Pass

The 104 carries the Trans-Canada Highway into the province from New Brunswick at Amherst and runs it east past Truro and New Glasgow to the Canso Causeway and Cape Breton. Its signature stretch is the Cobequid Pass — the high crossing of the Cobequid Mountains west of Truro, opened in the late 1990s as a modern bypass of the old shoreline road. For its first two decades the pass was a tolled section; the province began lifting the tolls in 2019. What hasn’t changed is the winter: the pass is the most weather-exposed piece of mainland Nova Scotia, and when a nor’easter stalls over the Bay of Fundy the province will close it outright — gates down, traffic held, sometimes for hours — rather than let vehicles stack up in zero visibility. If you’re driving the mainland in a winter storm, the Cobequid Pass is the stretch you check first.

The 101, the 103, and the 125

Three more corridors complete the picture. The 101 leaves Halifax westward through the Annapolis Valley — Windsor, Wolfville, Kentville, on toward Digby and Yarmouth — the province’s farm-country highway. The 103 takes the other coast, running the South Shore through Bridgewater and Liverpool toward Shelburne and Yarmouth, the road to Peggy’s Cove country and the ferry ports. And on Cape Breton, the 125 is the short expressway around Sydney — the island’s only km-marked highway. All three carry posted kilometre markers, and on the long rural stretches between exits, the marker is the only address there is.

The honest quirk: trunk roads and the Cabot Trail

Here’s the part that surprises visitors. Nova Scotia’s older highway generation — the trunk routes, Trunk 1, Trunk 2, Trunk 3 and their siblings — carries no posted kilometre markers at all. Neither does the Cabot Trail, the most famous drive in the province and one of the most famous in the country. Out there, navigation is landmark-style: the nearest community, the last lookoff, the park gate at Ingonish or Chéticamp. That’s workable on a road where the next village is ten minutes away, but it means a location report from the Trail has to lean on names, not numbers — “Cabot Trail northbound, about five kilometres past the Skyline trailhead” is the local dialect. If you want the precision of a posted marker, you have it on the 100-series and only on the 100-series.

Nova Scotia 511 and the winter reality

The province’s road-condition service is Nova Scotia 511 — closures, construction, and winter conditions reported by highway and location, plus the plow and camera coverage that matters from December through March. Nova Scotia winters are Atlantic winters: nor’easters that drop heavy wet snow and switch to rain and back, flash freezes behind the front, and wind that makes the high ground — the Cobequid Pass above all — a different storm than the coast. The reflex is the same one BC drivers have for the Coquihalla, scaled to the Maritimes: check 511 before you cross the pass in weather.

Breaking down on a Nova Scotia highway

If you break down or witness a crash on a 100-series highway, give the dispatcher three things: the highway number, your direction, and the last kilometre marker you passed. “Highway 104 eastbound, just past KM 22” puts help on the correct side of the median and within a minute of your bumper. On a trunk road or the Cabot Trail, fall back to the landmark dialect — community, junction, or business — and be as specific as the road lets you be. Either way: hazards on, get well clear of the travel lanes, and stay on the phone until dispatch confirms your location back to you.
