Highway Mile Markers in New York

July 4, 2026 4 min read

New York runs two marker systems side by side: the big green mileposts everyone recognizes, and the tiny stacked-number reference markers almost nobody can read. Here's how both work, how the Thruway numbers itself, and how to see your exact position plus live statewide alerts in real time.

How New York numbers its highways

On the interstates, New York signs standard green mileposts. But look at the shoulder of any state route and you'll spot something else: small green paddles with three stacked rows of numbers, roughly every tenth of a mile. Those are New York's reference markers, and they encode the route, the region, and mile information — a precise statewide location grid that most drivers pass a hundred times a day without ever learning to read. Then there's the NYS Thruway: I-87 and I-90 through the Thruway system carry their own milepost numbering that counts up from New York City, so a Thruway mile and a plain interstate mile aren't always the same scale.

The corridors people actually drive

  • I-90 (Thruway) is the spine — from the NYC end up past Albany, then west through Syracuse and Rochester to Buffalo.
  • I-87 runs the Thruway from the city to Albany, then the Adirondack Northway to the Canadian border.
  • I-81 comes up from Pennsylvania through Syracuse to the Thousand Islands.
  • I-88, I-86, and US-20 carry the traffic between and below.

Where mile markers matter most

Upstate distances surprise downstate drivers: it's over 400 Thruway miles from the NYC end to Buffalo, and long stretches of the Northway and I-81 run through genuinely remote country. Lake-effect snow off Erie and Ontario can drop feet — not inches — on I-90 and I-81 in hours, and when a travel ban or closure comes down, it's announced by milepost. A breakdown in a whiteout near Syracuse is exactly the moment you want your mile marker without hunting for a sign.

Lake-effect winters and downstate crawls

New York's road hazards come in two flavors. Upstate, it's lake-effect snow bands that bury one ten-mile stretch of I-90 while the next is bone dry, plus black ice on the Northway and spring flooding in the valleys. Downstate, it's density — construction, crashes, and closures on some of the busiest highway miles in the country. Either way, the official location language is the route and the mile.
