Highway Mile Markers in California

July 4, 2026 4 min read

Here's the thing most drivers never learn: California doesn't use continuous statewide mile markers at all. It uses postmiles — and they reset at every county line. Here's how to read them, where they matter most, and how to see your exact position plus live Caltrans and CHP alerts in real time.

How California numbers its highways

Instead of the green mileposts you see in most states, Caltrans marks its routes with postmiles: small white paddles showing the county abbreviation, the route number, and the mile. The catch is that the mile counts up from the county line, not the state line — so the numbers start over every time the route crosses into a new county. That means "mile 47 on US-395" exists in both Inyo County and Mono County, and the county on the paddle matters as much as the number. Newer freeways also carry continuous white exit-style markers, but the underlying system is county-based. MileCheck bundles California postmile data for 226 routes — around 8,100 points — and it works offline.

The corridors people actually drive

  • I-5 runs the full length of the state, from the Mexican border through Los Angeles, up the Central Valley past Sacramento, to the Oregon line.
  • US-101 follows the coast from LA through the Bay Area to the redwoods.
  • I-80 climbs from Sacramento over Donner Summit toward Reno — chain-control country every winter.
  • US-395 traces the Eastern Sierra past Lone Pine, Bishop, and Mammoth — long, remote, and the clearest illustration of the county-reset system.

Where the county reset bites

On a long rural route like US-395, the postmile reset is more than trivia. Tell a tow dispatcher you're at "mile 47 on 395" and you could be near Lone Pine in Inyo County or up past Crowley Lake in Mono County — nearly a hundred miles apart. Dispatchers and CHP officers know to ask for the county, but a stressed driver on a dark shoulder usually doesn't know it. An app that shows your exact route, county, and mile takes the guesswork out.

Chain controls, fires, and closures

California's roads get everything: chain controls over Donner Summit and Echo Summit every winter, fog in the Central Valley, rockslides on Highway 1, and fire closures that can shut long stretches of highway with little warning. Every one of those is announced by route and mile, and conditions can flip in an hour — which is exactly when a live alert beats a static map.

Everyday and emergency
