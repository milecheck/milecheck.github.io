Mile Markers vs. GPS: Why Both Still Matter on the Highway

June 28, 2026 Updated October 16, 2026 7 min read

"Why do I need mile markers when I have GPS?"

GPS helps locate your position. Mile markers identify a location along a highway.

A highway milepost can be easier to share than coordinates. On a highway, "I-84 eastbound near milepost 302" describes a route, direction and location. Google Maps, Apple Maps and Waze generally do not display your current milepost during navigation.

1. It's the language of the road

Mileposts are used in many highway reports and transportation records. A crash report may identify the highway, direction and milepost. A milepost can help dispatchers describe the location to responding crews. Many state DOT alerts identify crashes by milepost. Some local news reports use those locations too.

Other groups that move people around have their own version of this.

  • Railroads also use mileposts to identify locations along their lines.
  • Towboats on the Mississippi use river miles. Locks and bridges are called out by river mile on the radio.

Canadian highways also use kilometre-based distance references. MileCheck shows kilometre markers there too.

2. Doesn't my phone already tell 911 where I am?

Sort of. When you call 911, your phone sends an estimated location built from GPS satellites, cell towers and Wi-Fi. Often it's good. Sometimes it isn't. FCC rules set location-accuracy targets for wireless 911 calls. The location provided during an individual call can still be imprecise.

A location estimate may not make your travel direction or side of a divided highway clear. Tell the dispatcher where you are and which direction you are traveling. Virginia DOT puts it plainly: "When calling #77 or 911 for emergency assistance, providing the information on a mile marker sign will help responders reach you quickly." (More in the Virginia mile marker guide.)

What about texting 911? Not every 911 center can take texts yet. If yours can't, you get an automatic bounce-back telling you to call instead. Where texting does work, 911 centers tell you to put your location in the very first message. If you know your highway, direction and mile marker, put them in that first message. You can give the highway, direction and milepost in a short sentence.

3. How do road crews report where they are?

A road crew can report a highway location over radio. The shop radios, "Where are you?"

"395 southbound, milepost 112."

The route, direction and milepost give the listener a location to use. A radio report can share a highway location without sending a map link.

4. How do DOTs, police and the news use mileposts?

Highway agencies continue to use mileposts in their records. Many road records use mileposts alongside other location information.

  • DOT road alerts. Oregon's TripCheck lists incidents by highway and milepost, like "US26: MP 5.6 to 5.7, 3 miles West of Necanicum." WSDOT and 511 sites around the country work the same way.
  • Local news. Rural outlets like Elkhorn Media Group in Eastern Oregon report crashes, fires and closures as "Highway X near milepost Y." What else would they write? GPS coordinates mean nothing to readers, and a map screenshot is extra steps. "A crash on Cabbage Hill, around the second corner" leaves everyone asking which corner. The milepost settles it.
  • Police crash reports. Oregon's police crash report form has a box labeled "MILE POST" right next to county and highway. Washington State Patrol's collision manual tells officers to record the milepost, to the hundredth, when a crash is on a state highway and the milepost is known.
  • Your insurance claim. The crash report Oregon drivers file with the DMV has a "MILE POST" box next to the road. The report records a location that may also be relevant to an insurance claim. A route, direction and milepost can help identify where a reported incident occurred.
  • Federal rules. The FHWA requires states to keep a road network for all public roads that locates points by route and distance along the road.

A milepost makes an alert easy to place against where you are. Crash at milepost 255, and you're at 231. If you're headed toward higher mileposts, it's 24 miles ahead.

Sources: TripCheck road conditions · Oregon Police Traffic Crash Report (Form 46A) · WSP Collision Report Instruction Manual · Oregon DMV Accident and Insurance Report (735-32) · FHWA ARNOLD Reference Manual

5. In some places, the mile marker is the address

In the Florida Keys, many street numbers on US-1 come from the mile marker. Robbie's Marina is at 77522 Overseas Highway, which is mile 77.5. John Pennekamp Coral Reef State Park is at 102601 Overseas Highway, at mile marker 102.5. Locals use "bayside" and "oceanside" to identify which side of US 1 a place is on. (See the Florida mile marker guide.)

Along Alaska's highways, roadside businesses go by the highway mile. Sheep Mountain Lodge is at Mile 113.5 Glenn Highway, and the Alaska Wildlife Conservation Center is at Mile 79 Seward Highway. (See the Alaska guide.)

On North Carolina's Outer Banks, beach towns give directions by milepost. The Wright Brothers National Memorial is at milepost 7.5 on US-158, Jockey's Ridge State Park at milepost 12, and Jennette's Pier at milepost 16.5 on the Beach Road. (See the North Carolina guide.)

6. Where GPS gets shaky

Seattle's SR 99 tunnel runs about two miles under downtown, and it's built for emergencies. The SR 99 tunnel has cellular coverage and an emergency radio-override system.

What you can't count on is the dot on your map. Satellite signals have a hard time reaching underground, so your phone's GPS location can lag or drift. So the tunnel walls have signs showing the distance to the nearest emergency exit, and doors about every 650 feet lead to a refuge area and a separate exit corridor with its own ventilation.

A phone connection may be available inside the tunnel. Posted tunnel markers can provide another location reference. Follow tunnel emergency signs and instructions. (More in the Washington mile marker guide.)

Sources: WSDOT, SR 99 Tunnel · Seattle Met, where do the SR 99 tunnel exits go? · FOX 13, SR 99 tunnel safety features · The Seattle Times, SR 99 tunnel safety tech · KOMO News

7. Why did states add more mile markers?

Some states have put markers closer together to help emergency crews find incidents.

In 2011 Oregon DOT started testing half-mile markers on OR-217 and US-26, because troopers were rounding crash locations to the nearest mile marker. ODOT spokesman Don Hamilton told Fox News, "When the only reference on a highway is a mile-long stretch of road, emergency vehicles can only guess at the best on-ramp." (See the Oregon mile marker guide.) Massachusetts has used milepost markers at two-tenths-of-a-mile intervals on interstate highways (Massachusetts guide). North Carolina DOT told Fox News it had no plans to change how it installs markers despite enhanced 911, because "not everyone owns a GPS and they're not always accurate."

Sources: The Oregonian, Oregon starts test of half-mile markers (2011) · Boston Herald (2009) · Fox News, Your Tax Dollars Are Hitting the (Side of the) Road

8. Your map app won't show it

Google Maps, Apple Maps and Waze focus on navigation rather than displaying a driver's current highway milepost. On Google's own Maps help forum, someone asked how to get Google Maps to display mile markers. The answer from a Google Product Expert was to send Google a feature request, and more than 80 people have marked the same question. Many state 511 services focus on roads within their own state. After crossing a state line, you may need to check a different state's road information.

A navigation app may not show your current milepost, even when you have a data connection. And roadside markers are easy to miss at night, in snow, or right after a wreck.

Source: Google Maps Community, How can I get Google Maps to display mile markers?
