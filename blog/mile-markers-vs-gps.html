Mile Markers vs. GPS: Why Both Still Matter on the Highway

June 28, 2026 Updated October 16, 2026 7 min read

"Why do I need mile markers when I have GPS?"

Fair question. GPS gives you coordinates. A mile marker is the highway's street address.

Nobody tells the pizza place their latitude and longitude. They say "412 Main Street." On a highway, "I-84 eastbound, mile 302" is the address. Your phone knows the coordinates, but Google Maps, Apple Maps and Waze don't show mile markers.

1. It's the language of the road

Everyone who works the highway gives locations the same way. When a state trooper calls in a crash, they give the route, the direction and the milepost. Dispatch sends the ambulance and the tow truck to that milepost. The state DOT's road alerts list the crash by milepost, and the local news reports it the same way.

Other groups that move people around have their own version of this.

  • Railroads list every signal, switch and crossing by railroad milepost, and crews radio their location by milepost.
  • Towboats on the Mississippi use river miles. Locks and bridges are called out by river mile on the radio.
  • Pilots have GPS in the cockpit, and they still report "30 miles south of the Seattle VOR" (a radio beacon on the ground), not a string of coordinates. That's what the person on the other end understands.

Canada works the same way, in kilometres. MileCheck shows kilometre markers there too.

2. Doesn't my phone already tell 911 where I am?

Sort of. When you call 911, your phone sends an estimated location built from GPS satellites, cell towers and Wi-Fi. Often it's good. Sometimes it isn't. The FCC's rule only asks carriers to place 80% of wireless 911 calls within 50 meters, or at a dispatchable address, and highways are hard. The towers sit in a line along the road, which is the worst shape for pinpointing you. One Florida 911 coordinator described pings landing "3 houses down from where the caller is actually calling from."

Even when the dot is right, it doesn't tell the dispatcher which direction you're headed, which side of a divided highway you're on, or which on-ramp the ambulance should take. So they ask. Virginia DOT puts it plainly: "When calling #77 or 911 for emergency assistance, providing the information on a mile marker sign will help responders reach you quickly." (More in the Virginia mile marker guide.)

What about texting 911? Not every 911 center can take texts yet. If yours can't, you get an automatic bounce-back telling you to call instead. Where texting does work, 911 centers tell you to put your location in the very first message. So you'd be typing the mile marker anyway. Saying "I-90 westbound, mile 41" takes three seconds.

3. The radio call

Picture a road crew driver on a rural highway with one bar of signal, or none. The shop radios. "Where are you?"

"395 southbound, milepost 112."

That's the whole conversation. Without the mile marker, he'd be texting a GPS pin that might not send, might be off, and makes the person on the other end stop and open a map. Over a radio, a dispatcher check-in or a phone call, the mile marker is the address that works.

4. Every alert, report and form already uses it

Mile markers aren't a leftover from before GPS. They're how the highway keeps its records.

  • DOT road alerts. Open Oregon's TripCheck and look at a crash. "US26: MP 5.6 to 5.7, 3 miles West of Necanicum. Crash/Hazard." WSDOT and 511 sites around the country work the same way.
  • Local news. Rural outlets like Elkhorn Media Group in Eastern Oregon report crashes, fires and closures as "Highway X near milepost Y."
  • Police crash reports. Oregon's police crash report form has a box labeled "MILE POST" right next to county and highway. Washington State Patrol's collision manual tells officers to record the milepost, to the hundredth, when a crash is on a state highway and the milepost is known.
  • Your insurance claim. Oregon's crash report that drivers file with the DMV asks for the "highway and milepoint." That report is what your insurance company works from. "US-97 northbound, mile 152.4" is a location an adjuster can find and verify. "Somewhere outside Bend" is not. Same for a rock chip from a gravel truck or a deer hit.
  • Tow and roadside assistance. The first questions are always which highway, which direction, which mile marker. That decides which tow yard gets the call and what ETA you're given.
  • Federal law. Since 2014 the FHWA has required every state to keep milepost-referenced maps of all its public roads.

If all you have is a GPS dot, a TripCheck alert doesn't tell you much. If you know your mile marker, it's subtraction. Crash at milepost 255, and you're at 231. It's 24 miles ahead, so you have time to decide whether to stop, wait or reroute.

Sources: TripCheck road conditions · Oregon Police Traffic Crash Report (Form 46A) · WSP Collision Report Instruction Manual · Oregon DMV Accident and Insurance Report (735-32) · FHWA ARNOLD Reference Manual

5. In some places, the mile marker is the address

In the Florida Keys, street numbers on US-1 come straight from the mile marker. Robbie's Marina is at 77522 Overseas Highway, which is mile 77.5. John Pennekamp Coral Reef State Park is at 102601 Overseas Highway, at mile marker 102.5. Locals add "bayside" or "oceanside" and you know exactly where to go. (See the Florida mile marker guide.)

Along Alaska's highways, roadside businesses go by the highway mile. Sheep Mountain Lodge is at Mile 113.5 Glenn Highway, and the Alaska Wildlife Conservation Center is at Mile 79 Seward Highway. (See the Alaska guide.)

On North Carolina's Outer Banks, beach towns give directions by milepost. The Wright Brothers National Memorial is at milepost 7.5 on US-158, Jockey's Ridge State Park at milepost 12, and Jennette's Pier at milepost 16.5 on the Beach Road. (See the North Carolina guide.)

6. Where GPS gets shaky

Seattle's SR 99 tunnel runs about two miles under downtown, and it's built for emergencies. Cell service works inside, and WSDOT can override FM radio to broadcast instructions during an incident. You can make the call down there.

What you can't count on is the dot on your map. Satellite signals have a hard time reaching underground, so your phone's GPS location can lag or drift. So the tunnel has reference signs along the walls, and emergency doors about every 650 feet that lead to a refuge area and a separate exit corridor with its own ventilation. If you break down inside, the sign next to you tells responders exactly where you are and which door is closest.

The phone works. The sign on the wall is what tells them where you are. (More in the Washington mile marker guide.)

Sources: WSDOT, SR 99 Tunnel · Seattle Met, where do the SR 99 tunnel exits go? · FOX 13, SR 99 tunnel safety features · The Seattle Times, SR 99 tunnel safety tech · KOMO News

7. States keep adding more of them

If mile markers were obsolete, the spending would have stopped. It hasn't.

In 2011 Oregon DOT started testing half-mile markers on OR-217 and US-26, because troopers were rounding crash locations to the nearest mile marker. ODOT spokesman Don Hamilton told Fox News, "When the only reference on a highway is a mile-long stretch of road, emergency vehicles can only guess at the best on-ramp." (See the Oregon mile marker guide.) Massachusetts went further, with markers every two-tenths of a mile on its interstates (Massachusetts guide). North Carolina DOT told Fox News it had no plans to change how it installs markers despite enhanced 911, because "not everyone owns a GPS and they're not always accurate."

Sources: The Oregonian, Oregon starts test of half-mile markers (2011) · Boston Herald (2009) · Fox News, Your Tax Dollars Are Hitting the (Side of the) Road

8. Your map app won't show it

Google Maps, Apple Maps and Waze are built for turn-by-turn directions, and they don't show mile markers. On Google's own Maps help forum, someone asked how to get Google Maps to display mile markers. The answer from a Google Product Expert was to send Google a feature request, and more than 80 people have marked the same question. State 511 apps show alerts by milepost, but only for their own state. Cross a state line and you need a different app.

So even with full signal and a map open, most drivers can't read off their mile marker. And the signs on the roadside are a mile apart and easy to miss at night, in snow, or right after a wreck.

Source: Google Maps Community, How can I get Google Maps to display mile markers?

So where does that leave you?

GPS is incredible. It's also not what gets a tow truck or an ambulance to you on a specific stretch of a specific highway. That still comes down to a person saying where they are, out loud, in the language the road uses.
