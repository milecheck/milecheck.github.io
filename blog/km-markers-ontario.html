Kilometre Markers in Ontario: A Driver’s Guide

July 12, 2026 5 min read

Cross from Michigan or New York into Ontario and the numbers change on you: speed limits in km/h, distances in kilometres, and no little green mileposts ticking by every mile. But Ontario has a location system every bit as usable as an American milepost line — it just hides in plain sight. On Ontario’s freeways, the exit numbers are the kilometre markers. Here’s how the system works, and how it’s coming to MileCheck.

Exit numbers ARE the km-posts

Ontario numbers its freeway exits by kilometre distance from the highway’s starting point. That single decision turns every exit sign into a position report. On Highway 401, Exit 369 at Yonge Street in Toronto sits 369 kilometres from the Windsor end. Pass Exit 410 and you know you’ve covered 41 kilometres since Yonge Street — no math beyond subtraction. Behind the scenes, Ontario’s Ministry of Transportation references its highways through a linear referencing system (LHRS), but that’s an internal bookkeeping tool. The kilometre count a driver can actually see and use is the exit numbering.

The 401: one highway, most of the province

Highway 401 runs from Windsor at the Detroit border to the Quebec border east of Cornwall, and its exit-kilometre count runs with it — from single digits at Windsor to numbers past 800 at the far end. Through Toronto it widens into one of the busiest stretches of road in North America, carrying roughly 400,000 vehicles a day at its peak. That density is exactly why the km-based exits matter: when traffic reporting says “collision on the 401 near Exit 369,” every driver on the corridor knows precisely where that is and how far away they are from it.

The 400-series and the QEW

Ontario’s freeway network is the 400-series: Highway 400 north from Toronto toward cottage country, the 401 across the south, the 403 and 404 and 410 threading the Greater Toronto Area, the 407 toll route arcing around the top of the city, and the 417 through Ottawa. All use kilometre-based exit numbering. Two of them are worth a note. The 407 is a toll road with all-electronic tolling — no booths, cameras read your plate — so budget for the bill if you take it around Toronto. And the odd one out is the QEW — the Queen Elizabeth Way, the Toronto–Niagara freeway. It carries a name instead of a number, runs roughly 139 kilometres, and numbers its exits by kilometre from the Fort Erie end at the US border. Same system, no route number.

The north: Highways 11 and 17

Southern Ontario is dense freeway country, but most of the province by area is the opposite: Highway 17 along Lake Superior and Highway 11 arcing further north carry the Trans-Canada through hundreds of kilometres of rock, forest, and very little else. Between Sault Ste. Marie and Thunder Bay you can drive for a long stretch without fuel or cell signal, and the two-lane sections close hard in winter storms. Out there, a location report has to lean on the highway number and distance from the last town or junction — exactly the kind of driving where a live kilometre readout stops being a convenience and starts being safety equipment.

King’s Highways — and where Highway 2 went

Below the freeways sit the two-digit King’s Highways — routes like Highway 7, 11, and 17, the last two carrying the Trans-Canada across the province’s enormous north. If you go looking for the old Highway 2, the historic Windsor–Montreal road, you’ll mostly come up empty: in the late-1990s Ontario transferred long stretches of its older highways to municipalities, and most of Highway 2 stopped being a provincial highway at all. It survives as local roads with local names. On the routes the province still runs, the highway number plus the nearest exit or interchange remains the way locations get reported.

Closures, squalls, and Ontario 511

Ontario’s traffic operations run through the COMPASS freeway management system and the public Ontario 511 service, which reports closures, collisions, and construction by highway and location. Winter is when it earns its keep: snow squalls rolling off the Great Lakes can drop visibility to nothing in minutes, and highways like the 400 north of Toronto and the 402 west of London have been closed outright by squalls more than once. When the OPP closes a stretch of freeway, the closure is described the way everything in Ontario is — by highway and interchange.

The same convention works in reverse when you’re the one calling. Break down or witness a collision, and the most useful report you can give — to 911, to the OPP, to a tow dispatcher — is three things: the highway, your direction, and the last exit number you passed. Because Ontario’s exits are kilometre-based, that last item is a position accurate to a few kilometres, which on an 800-kilometre highway is the difference between help finding you and help searching for you.

What this means in MileCheck

MileCheck was built on a simple idea: you should always know exactly where you are on the highway, in the reference system the road actually uses. In the US that’s the mile marker. In Ontario it’s the kilometre — and Ontario km-post support is coming to MileCheck in an upcoming update. When it lands, the app will show your live position as highway plus kilometre — Hwy 401 · KM 369 — the same way the exit signs, the traffic reports, and the tow dispatcher already talk. If you drive the 401, the QEW, or anything in the 400-series, it’ll speak your road’s language.
