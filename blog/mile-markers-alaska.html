Highway Mile Markers in Alaska

July 4, 2026 4 min read

Nowhere in America do mile markers matter more than Alaska. The highways have names instead of numbers, the mileposts double as street addresses, and in winter, knowing exactly where you are isn't a convenience — it's survival planning. Here's how it works.

How Alaska numbers its highways — it mostly doesn't

Alaskans know their roads by name: the Glenn, the Parks, the Seward, the Richardson, the Sterling. Route numbers exist on paper, but nobody uses them. Instead, locations are given as mileposts along the named highway — "Mile 99 Glenn Highway" is a real address, the kind that appears on mailboxes, lodge signs, and trailhead directions. Mile marker data for Alaska's highways is bundled into MileCheck and works offline, which matters in a state where the signal disappears for an hour at a time.

The corridors people actually drive

  • The Glenn Highway runs northeast from Anchorage through Palmer toward Glennallen.
  • The Parks Highway connects Anchorage and Fairbanks past Denali.
  • The Seward Highway follows Turnagain Arm south from Anchorage to Seward on the Kenai Peninsula.
  • The Richardson Highway links Valdez to Fairbanks; the Sterling Highway carries fishing traffic to Soldotna and Homer.

The Alaska Highway counts from Dawson Creek

The Alaska Highway enters the state at the Port Alcan crossing and ends 200 miles later at Delta Junction, where it meets the Richardson. Its mileposts do not start at the border. They continue a count that began at Mile 0 in Dawson Creek, British Columbia, when the road was built in 1942 as 1,422 miles of gravel. The border crossing is about Mile 1222, Tok is Mile 1314, and the last post is Mile 1422 in Delta Junction. On the Canadian side the same count runs in kilometres, so Whitehorse is about km 1425 and the Yukon–Alaska line about km 1900. The road has been shortened by roughly 35 miles since 1942, so these are historical miles, and a kilometre post in the Yukon and a milepost in Alaska never convert into each other exactly. Give the number on the post. It is the address system that road runs on.

The Seward Highway counts down

Here's the quirk visitors miss: the Seward Highway is measured from Seward, so the mileposts count down as you drive south from Anchorage — roughly milepost 127 in town, dropping through Girdwood around mile 90 and Moose Pass near mile 29, to milepost 0 at Seward. Locals give directions accordingly: the scenic pull-offs along Turnagain Arm, the Girdwood turnoff, the combat-fishing holes — all by milepost, all counting toward zero.

Winter driving where position is survival

Alaska winters make position reporting non-optional. Black ice, avalanche zones along Turnagain Arm, moose on the road in the dark, and temperatures that make waiting for help a serious matter — when something goes wrong, "Mile 71 Glenn, northbound side" is the difference between a fast response and a search. Alaska's winter road reports describe conditions by highway and milepost, so the mileposts are the language the whole system speaks.

Everyday and emergency

Whether you're giving directions to a lodge at Mile 99 Glenn Highway, reporting a moose strike on the Parks, or watching the ice reports before Turnagain Arm, the milepost is the location Alaska runs on — literally, as an address.
