Are There More Crashes on a Full Moon?

November 26, 2026 6 min read

This week's full moon was a supermoon, the Beaver Moon of November 24. It helped push king tides over coastal roads from Washington to San Diego. It also raises an old question people love to argue about: do more people crash when the moon is full?

For most of us it's folklore. But one team of researchers actually ran the numbers, using 40 years of fatal crash records from the US government. The answer is a small, careful yes, for one group of drivers.

The study: 40 years of full moons

In December 2017, Donald Redelmeier of the University of Toronto and Eldar Shafir of Princeton published a study in The BMJ called "The full moon and motorcycle related mortality." They used the federal Fatality Analysis Reporting System, the national registry of every fatal crash on a public road, from 1975 through 2014.

They looked only at the evening and overnight hours, 4 p.m. to 8 a.m. They compared 494 full-moon nights with 988 control nights: the same night of the week, one week before and one week after each full moon. Using nearby nights as the comparison helps cancel out things like season, weather patterns and holiday weekends.

Source: The BMJ, Redelmeier and Shafir (2017)

What they found

  • Full-moon nights: 4,494 fatal motorcycle crashes, or 9.10 per night.
  • Control nights: 8,535 fatal motorcycle crashes, or 8.64 per night.
  • The difference: about 5% higher risk on a full-moon night (relative risk 1.05, 95% confidence interval 1.02 to 1.09).

That works out to roughly one extra fatal motorcycle crash for every two full moons in the US, or 226 over the 40 years. It's real enough to show up across four decades of data. It's also small. The vast majority of full-moon nights look like any other night on the road.

Supermoons: about 22% higher

A supermoon is a full moon that happens when the moon is near its closest point to Earth. It can look up to 14% larger and 30% brighter than the smallest full moons of the year, though most people can barely tell the difference by eye.

The researchers split out 65 supermoon nights. Those nights averaged 10.82 fatal motorcycle crashes, compared with 8.84 on ordinary full-moon nights. That's about 22% higher (relative risk 1.22, 95% confidence interval 1.13 to 1.33).

Sources: The BMJ, Redelmeier and Shafir (2017) · National Geographic

A clever check: the same days, in daylight

If something else about those dates explained the pattern, you might expect daytime crashes to rise too. They didn't. On the same dates between 8 a.m. and 4 p.m., when the moon isn't the thing in the sky, there was no increase (relative risk 0.95, not statistically significant).

The authors also checked the United Kingdom, Canada and Australia. The numbers pointed the same way, about 8% higher, but there were too few crashes for that result to be statistically significant on its own.

Why would the moon matter?

The researchers' leading idea is distraction. A big, bright moon rising over the horizon is exactly the kind of thing that pulls your eyes off the road, and on a motorcycle a glance at the wrong moment leaves very little room for error.

They're careful to say that isn't proven. They list other possible explanations: more people out at night, faster speeds, longer trips, more cross traffic, and moonlight changing how riders judge distance and speed.

What the study doesn't show

  • It says nothing about cars. The authors write that they did not analyze automobiles. Don't take this as proof that a full moon makes car crashes more likely.
  • It can't see the sky. There was no data on cloud cover or whether the moon was even visible.
  • It's observational. It finds a pattern. It can't rule out a hidden cause the data doesn't capture.
  • The effect is small. The authors say the effect is small and the confidence intervals are wide.

So the honest summary is this: in one large, well-designed study, riders were a little more likely to die in a crash on full-moon nights, and more so on supermoons. Nobody has shown the same thing for the rest of us.

The next supermoons land in holiday travel

The November 24 Beaver Moon starts a run of supermoons this winter. The next one, the Cold Moon, is full on December 23, 2026 at 8:28 p.m. Eastern, and it's the closest full moon of the year. It will be rising in the evening, two days before Christmas, while a lot of people are on the road. The one after that is January 22, 2027 at 7:17 a.m. Eastern.

Source: Farmers' Almanac

What to do with this

The authors' advice for riders is simple: take extra care on full-moon nights, and stay alert the rest of the time too. Ride with your headlight on, wear a helmet and protective gear, scan for road defects, respect the weather and watch for left-turning vehicles.

For everyone else, the takeaway is the same one that applies every night: if the moon is stunning, pull over somewhere safe and look at it there. And before a night drive, especially on a holiday, it's worth a minute to check what's on your route.
