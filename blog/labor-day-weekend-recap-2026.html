Highway Report

What the roads actually did over Labor Day weekend

September 8, 2026

Before the weekend we published what was scheduled. This is the other half: what the road actually did. We saved a copy of every state DOT feed plus British Columbia once a day, Thursday September 3 through Labor Day Monday, and this is what those 10,951 records show.

How to read these numbers

One thing matters more than any figure below, so it goes first.

This is a daily photograph, not a video. We captured each jurisdiction once every afternoon, around 1 PM Pacific. A crash that happened at 8 PM Saturday and was cleared by midnight never appears here. So every count below is a floor — the least that was happening, not the most.

States do not report alike. California posted 1,500 records over five days. Rhode Island posted none at all. That gap is mostly about how much detail each DOT publishes, not how much was happening on the ground, so the state-by-state numbers are not a fair ranking of anything.

Records are counted once, by their incident ID, no matter how many days they appear.

The weekend in one screen

September 3–7, 2026 — 51 jurisdictions, five daily snapshots
  • 10,951 distinct records
  • 7,631 construction · 1,971 closures
  • 360 crashes visible — a floor, not a total (see below)
  • 832 active wildfires burning 5,948,187 acres
  • 219 records mentioning snow or ice — in September

The crash number is the shakiest thing here

Twenty-six of the 50 jurisdictions that posted anything showed no crashes at all. The tempting conclusion is that their feeds do not carry crashes. We went and checked instead, because a crash is a short-lived thing and we only look once a day — a wreck cleared in an hour has roughly a one-in-twenty chance of being on screen when we take the picture.

The checking turned up three different answers.

Most of those states really do publish no crashes. North Carolina posted 3,177 records over the weekend — every one of them roadwork or a closure, not a single collision. Missouri posted 1,072, Idaho 586, Illinois 266, all with the same shape. Those feeds carry work zones, not wrecks.

Two of them were our fault. Oregon does report crashes — it files them under a vehicle-incident type with text reading “A crash has occurred.” Minnesota reports them too, tagged as generic events. Our counting script only recognised crashes carrying the standard code, so it read both states as crash-free. Our own app catches them correctly; the reporting script had drifted from it. We found that while fact-checking this article and fixed it.

And the sampling limit is real everywhere else. Even California, which reports crashes diligently, had just sixteen on its feed at the moment we last looked — against nine thousand other records. Whatever the true crash count for the weekend was, it is far above 360, and we cannot tell you by how much.

So take the numbers below as the smallest possible version of the weekend, from the 24 places whose feeds we can currently read for crashes:

Crashes visible in five daily snapshots, Sept 3–7
  • CA — 81
  • NY — 43
  • AL — 37
  • WI — 27
  • GA — 24
  • FL — 19

That is a publishing ranking, not a safety ranking. It says which states write crashes down and how often we happened to be looking — nothing about which roads were dangerous.

The West was on fire

While most of the country was driving to a barbecue, 832 wildfires were burning 5,948,187 acres — an area larger than New Jersey. It was not close to evenly spread:

Active fires over the holiday weekend
  • OR — 111 fires, 2,911,280 acres
  • BC — 88 fires, 1,026,836 acres
  • WA — 61 fires, 665,217 acres
  • ID — 33 fires, 209,267 acres
  • UT — 16 fires, 198,213 acres
  • NV — 12 fires, 164,040 acres

Oregon alone accounted for 49% of the burning acreage across every state and province we track. Our own alert feeds carried 47 records mentioning fire over the same five days — the roadside sliver of something much bigger.

Snow. In September.

219 records mentioned snow or ice over a weekend most people spent at the beach. Mountain passes do not care what the calendar says — and this is the weekend the season turns on them.

I-80 tops it, which is what you would expect from a road that crosses eleven states. See any of them live on our I-80 map, or browse every corridor.

The closures that will outlast this article

Buried in a holiday weekend's data are projects measured in years, not hours:

Longest-running closures still standing
  • WI I-535 — 2,616 days, through 2029-06-06
  • TX US-87 — 2,070 days, through 2031-10-31
  • WA SR-165 — 1,902 days, through 2030-07-01
  • NC US-17 — 1,859 days, through 2027-12-31
  • NY I-91 — 1,733 days, through 2028-12-01

Washington's SR-165 has been shut at the Carbon River bridge to everyone — cars, bikes, walkers — since April 2025. It is not scheduled to reopen until 2030. A child who started kindergarten when it closed will be in fifth grade.

What we could not see

Worth saying plainly, because it shapes everything above.

We only looked once a day. Rhode Island's feed returned nothing usable for all five days, so it contributes zero records and that is a gap in our data, not an empty road. New Mexico's feed was down when we checked before the weekend. And none of this covers live traffic — the jam that built at 4 PM and cleared by 6 is invisible to a daily snapshot.

What it is good for is the shape of a holiday weekend: where the work was, where the fires were, which roads carried the load. That is a picture no state publishes on its own, because no state sees past its own border.
