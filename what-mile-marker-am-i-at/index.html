    How does this work?

    Your browser finds your location after you allow access. MileCheck compares it with recorded DOT mileposts. It shows a nearby post, not an exact position between posts. Nearby roads, missing records and location accuracy can affect the result.

    Finding the number without a phone

    Mile markers are usually small green signs with white numbers. Nevada's dispatch guide notes that some around Las Vegas are white with black numbers. Look on the right shoulder first. Where there is no room on the right, some are in the median. They can sit up to 30 feet from the pavement, so scan past the guardrail.

    The count starts at the south or west state line, or where the route begins, and climbs as you head north or east. The sign for the other direction sits directly across the road and shows the same number. On major highways they come every mile. At 60 mph that is one a minute, so if you missed one, watch for the next.

    Two markers in a row also tell you your direction. If the numbers are going up, you are heading north or east on that route. If they are going down, you are heading south or west.

    Some states add markers every tenth, two tenths or half mile, so you may read 42.3 or 42.5. Others post enhanced markers with the direction on top and the route shield under it. One of those tells you the highway, the direction and the mile in a single glance.

    Use the exit number

    Exit numbers come from the same count. Federal sign rules say a crossroad that meets the highway between mile 15 and mile 16 should be exit 15. If you can't see a marker, give dispatch the last exit you passed, your direction, and about how far you have gone since. A letter after the number, as in 15A and 15B, means more than one exit shares that mile number. They can be ramps at one interchange or separate interchanges close together. The letters run in alphabetical order in the direction the numbers climb, so heading the other way you reach 15B before 15A. On a beltway, the MUTCD says exits are numbered clockwise, starting near the south end of the loop.

    Directions written before a state switched to mile-based numbering use the old exit numbers. Georgia switched in 1999 and 2000. Florida switched most of its interstates in 2002. The signs on the road are current.

    What to tell dispatch

    The mile marker is the first thing dispatch asks for on a highway. Nevada's highway patrol dispatch guide asks callers for these.

    • The highway. The route number, such as I-15 or US-95.
    • The marker or exit. The one you are near or just passed.
    • The direction. The way you are heading, or the way the vehicle or hazard is going.

    The guide's own example is "I-15 northbound passing milemarker 17." For debris or a stalled car, it asks which lane, counting the far-left lane as lane one. It also notes that 911 will transfer you to the right agency. If you are driving, pull over before you call, or hand the phone to a passenger.

    In Florida, *FHP (*347) reaches the Florida Highway Patrol to report a crash or a stranded driver. For more on the call itself, read what to tell 911 after a highway breakdown.

    If no marker is in sight

    On a two-lane road, the MUTCD lets a state post markers on one side only, sometimes back to back on one post. Your number may be on the far side of the road.

    In a city, use the cross street or exit name. Nevada's dispatch guide gives "US-95 southbound passing Rainbow" as a good report. A rest area or the last town you passed also helps. Say which way you are heading and whether the landmark is behind you or ahead.

    Why the lookup can be off

    GPS.gov says GPS-enabled phones are typically accurate to about 16 feet under open sky. Buildings and tree cover make it worse. On a computer without GPS, the browser falls back on whatever the device offers, such as Wi-Fi location, which is less exact.

    This page shows the nearest recorded milepost, not a point between two posts. Near an interchange, the closest post can belong to the road crossing over you. If the route shown doesn't match the shield on the road, trust the sign. What highway am I on? can help confirm it.

    Where the numbers reset

    Interstate mile numbers generally restart at state lines, and some reference systems restart at county lines. Mile 5 in Kentucky and mile 5 in Ohio are about 190 miles apart on I-75, so name the state when you are near a border.

    A few places count differently. California posts postmiles that restart at each county line, so a postmile needs its county. In Nevada, green interstate markers count from the state line, while white and black mileposts count from the county line and carry a county code such as CL for Clark. Where two highways share a road, the count continues for only one of them, and an interstate keeps its numbers over a US or state route. On Texas state highways, the small green three-digit placards are TxDOT reference markers, posted every 2 miles, or every mile on interstates.
