Driving in the US as a foreign visitor: what nobody explains to you

Renting a car in the US is easy. Understanding what "mile marker 142" means, why the next town is 90 minutes away, or what an American 911 dispatcher actually needs from you — nobody hands you that at the rental counter. Here's what's different, and what to know before you're the one stuck on the shoulder.

The US doesn't use kilometre posts — it uses mile markers

Most of the world marks distance with a kilometre post. American highways use a small green sign called a mile marker (or milepost), planted roughly every mile along the shoulder. It resets to 0 at the state line, or at the start of the highway, and counts up from there — so the same highway can read "142" for a hundred miles through one state and reset entirely at the next border.

It isn't decorative. It's how a stranded driver, in a country with genuinely enormous distances between towns, tells someone exactly where they are. More on how mile markers work →

What to say if you call 911

In much of Europe, dialling the emergency number from a smartphone sends your GPS position automatically. That is far less reliable across the US — rural counties, older 911 systems, and patchy cell coverage mean the dispatcher often cannot see your location at all. They will ask you to describe where you are, and the fastest possible answer is three things, in this order:

  1. The route — "I-40," "US-93," "State Route 87."
  2. The direction you're travelling — northbound, southbound, eastbound, westbound.
  3. The nearest mile marker — the number on the small green sign you just passed.
Those three facts pin your location on a highway that may run for a thousand miles, with no other reference point in sight. More on reporting your location →

Distances are bigger than they look on a map

A US state can be the size of a small European country. "Just a few inches on the map" can mean two hours with nothing but highway — no town, no fuel station, sometimes no cell signal. Fill up well before the tank looks low, and don't assume the next exit has services just because there is a next exit.

Speed limits and signs work differently

US speed limits are posted in miles per hour, not kilometres per hour — a 70 mph sign is about 113 km/h, easy to misread at a glance if you're used to metric. Distance signs (to the next town, the next exit) are also in miles. Every rental dashboard in the US displays mph by default; if yours has a settings menu, most modern cars let you switch the digital display to km/h even though the posted signs stay in miles — know which one you're reading.

What a state trooper wants when you're pulled over

If a police vehicle with flashing lights comes up behind you, pull over to the right shoulder and stop — don't panic and stop in the lane. Turn off the engine, keep your hands visible on the wheel, and wait for the officer to approach. Your passport and rental agreement (or International Driving Permit, if your license requires one) should be reachable without digging through the car. It's normal, not alarming — American troopers patrol highways routinely and most stops are for a minor traffic matter.

Laws that change at the state line

The US doesn't have one national traffic code — each state sets its own, and a few catch visitors off guard because they're the opposite of what "most states" do.

  • Turning right on red: allowed almost everywhere after a full stop, unless a sign says otherwise. New York City is the reverse — right on red is banned in all five boroughs unless a sign specifically permits it.
  • Pumping your own gas: normal everywhere except New Jersey, where self-serve is illegal statewide — an attendant pumps it for you, and they expect you to stay in the car.
  • Radar detectors: legal to carry in most states, but illegal in Virginia and Washington, D.C. even switched off in the glovebox. Illegal for commercial drivers everywhere.
  • Drink-driving limit: 0.08% blood alcohol almost nationwide — except Utah, at 0.05%, the strictest in the country.
  • Cannabis: legal for adults in many states, but it is illegal everywhere to drive under its influence, illegal to carry it across a state line even between two legal states, and illegal on federal land (national parks, military bases) regardless of state law.
  • Cell phones: most states now ban holding a phone at all while driving; a handful only ban texting. If your rental doesn't have hands-free built in, assume holding the phone is illegal until you check.
One rule genuinely is the same everywhere: the move-over law. Every state requires slowing down and changing lanes away from any vehicle stopped on the shoulder with flashing lights — police, tow truck, or a driver with hazards on. It's the law most visitors have never heard of and the one most likely to matter on a long highway drive.

The one number worth saving before you drive

911 is the emergency number nationwide — police, fire, medical, all one number, and it works from any phone including a rental's, even without a US SIM. For a breakdown that isn't an emergency, your rental agreement's own roadside-assistance number comes first — save it before you leave the counter, not after you need it.

Questions, answered

What is a mile marker on a US highway?

A small green sign posted roughly every mile along the shoulder of a US highway, showing the distance along that road from its starting point or the state line. It's the main way American drivers — and 911 dispatchers — describe an exact location on a long highway.

What should I tell 911 if I don't know exactly where I am in the US?

Give the route number, your direction of travel, and the nearest mile marker — in that order. Unlike in much of Europe, US 911 often cannot see your phone's GPS location automatically, especially in rural areas, so this is usually the fastest way to be found.

Are US speed limits in miles or kilometres?

Miles per hour. A posted "70" means 70 mph, roughly 113 km/h. Distance signs to the next town or exit are also in miles, not kilometres.

Do I need an International Driving Permit to drive in the US?

It depends on your home country and the state you're driving in — some accept a valid foreign licence alone, others expect an International Driving Permit alongside it. Check before you travel, and keep both with your rental agreement where you can reach them quickly.

What do I do if I get pulled over by police in the US?

Pull onto the right shoulder, stop, turn off the engine, and keep your hands visible on the wheel. Wait for the officer to approach before reaching for documents. It's a routine, common occurrence on American highways.

Do US traffic laws vary by state?

Yes — there's no single national code. Most rules are similar, but a few flip entirely: New York City bans right turns on red where most of the country allows them, New Jersey requires an attendant to pump your gas, and Utah's drink-driving limit (0.05%) is stricter than the 0.08% used almost everywhere else.

Can I turn right on red in the US?

Generally yes, after a full stop, unless a sign says otherwise — except in New York City, where it's banned in all five boroughs unless specifically signed to allow it.

What is the move-over law in the US?

Every US state requires drivers to slow down and change lanes away from any vehicle stopped on the shoulder with flashing or hazard lights — police, tow trucks, road crews, or another driver's breakdown. It applies everywhere, unlike most other traffic rules that vary by state.
