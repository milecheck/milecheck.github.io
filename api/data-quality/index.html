How fresh and complete is the MileCheck road alert data?

Published November 1, 2026 · Updated November 4, 2026

Read from the live API on November 4, 2026 at 10:28 AM Pacific: 49 of 55 states and provinces reporting live, 22,693 alerts in all.

How a road event gets to your software

Something happens on the road→The agency verifies it
1. The state DOT posts itFederal rule 23 CFR 511.309 gives agencies 10 minutes from verifying a lane-blocking incident to post it in metro areas, and 20 minutes outside them, on Interstates and other major routes they designate.
→Within 5 minutes in 47 states and provinces, 15 minutes in 7
2. MileCheck reads the feedWe check each agency on a set interval, add the mile marker, and put every place in one format. The table below lists the interval for each place.
→On your next call
3. Your software gets itEach API call returns what we hold at that moment.

Weather closures follow the same 20-minute rule from when the agency observes them. Construction closures run 10 minutes in metro areas and 20 outside.

What each column means

  • Alerts now. Records the API returns for that place at the read time, after we drop records with no numbered highway.
  • With a DOT mile marker. The share of those records where the agency itself sent a mile marker. The API adds an estimated-mile-marker field, labeled as ours, where the agency sent none.
  • Newest record. How long ago the agency last touched any one record. A quiet road can show an old number while the feed is fine, so read it as a hint, not a delay.
  • We read the agency. The interval we have set in the Worker. It is how often we check, not how fast the agency posts.

How soon we see a crash after the agency's own time

From September 26, 2026 to November 4, 2026 we logged when each crash and road hazard first appeared in an agency's feed and compared it with the start time the agency put on the record. Half of the records in each state reached us within the first number below. Three in four reached us within the second. The time includes how long the agency took to post the record and the gap before our next check, which is 15 minutes or less in every place.

A place is left out when it had fewer than 30 usable records or when its start times do not mark when the event happened: Indiana, Kentucky, Manitoba, Minnesota, Nebraska, New Hampshire, North Dakota, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas.

What this page does not say

  • It does not time each record from the road to your software. The table above covers the agency-to-us part for crashes and hazards, and the steps above show the limit each step works within.
  • It does not promise uptime. We do not publish a service level agreement. The status page shows current service.
  • One read is one moment. Roadwork counts swing by day of the week, and an agency can be down when you look.

Our mission

Become the trusted source for road information across North America, from today’s closures to next season’s roadwork.

What we do

We put every state’s Department of Transportation data in one place, on your phone, in your car and in your browser.

Our values

  • No ads in the app. Our website is free to use, so it has a clearly marked sponsor slot to help pay for it, without pop-ups.
  • Road data, not your data. We collect road data from state DOTs, not data about you. You don’t need an account, your mile marker is calculated on your phone, your trip history stays there, and we never sell your data.
  • Only what’s needed. Weather sends your approximate location to the National Weather Service, route planning sends your location and destination to Google, and wildfire alerts send a location rounded to about 7 miles only if you turn them on. Sentry receives anonymous crash reports, we count feature use without location or identity, and Apple or Google handles subscriptions.
  • Built for real roads. Nearly every feature exists because someone on a real road asked for it.
